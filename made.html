How this game
was made

Battle for Independence is a 3D fighting game built entirely with Three.js, WebGL, and a procedurally generated fighter pipeline. This page walks through how it was built — and lets you take the fighters apart.

No coding background? Start here

If terms like rig, GLB, or animation clip don't mean anything to you, open this section first. The short version of everything is below; the technical deep-dive is in the sections after.

The 30-second version.

A 3D model is just a hollow shell made of triangles. To make a fighter move, we glue an invisible skeleton inside the shell, then teach each bone how to bend. Each move the fighter can do — a punch, a crouch, a knockdown — is a short recording of how every bone should rotate. The game stitches those recordings together when you press buttons.

modelWhat even is a 3D model?

A 3D model is a hollow shape built out of tiny triangles. The figure you're looking at in the viewer on the right is roughly 150,000 triangles stitched together so they look like a person. The triangles carry three things: where their corners sit in space (position), which way they face (normals), and their colour (paint).

Picture a beach-ball made of coloured paper plates glued edge to edge. Now shrink each plate to about 3 mm and stack 150,000 of them. That's roughly a 3D model.

meshWhy does it look "blocky" up close?

Because it really is. There are no smooth surfaces underneath — just triangles. When you're far away, your eye fills in the gaps. When you zoom in (or pull the fighter apart with the explode slider), the flat plates become visible. The number of triangles is a trade-off: more triangles = smoother curves but slower to render.

rigWhat does "rigging" mean?

Rigging is putting a digital skeleton inside the model. The skeleton is a tree of bones — hips at the bottom, spine on top, then chest, neck, head, and from the chest, two arms branching into shoulder, upper arm, forearm, hand, and from the hips, two legs into upper leg, knee, foot, toe.

Each bone is invisibly glued to a chunk of the surrounding triangles, so when the bone rotates, the triangles move with it. The rig for both fighters in this game has 25 bones.

clipWhat is an animation clip?

An animation clip is a short, time-stamped recording of how every bone in the skeleton should rotate. A "jab" clip might say: at frame 0 the right arm is at the hip; at frame 4 the right arm is fully extended; at frame 12 the arm is back at the hip.

The game plays these recordings on demand. Press a punch button, the jab clip plays. Press block, the guard clip loops. Clips can be blended — that's how a fighter can be part-way through a punch and start a kick at the same time.

shaderWhat's a "shader"?

A shader is a tiny program that runs once per triangle, on the graphics card, to decide what colour to draw. The "key light" you can see hitting the fighter's shoulder? That's a shader working out where the light hits and how bright it should be.

This game uses fairly simple shaders (the standard "physical" material that ships with Three.js). The fancy stuff — reflections, glow, soft shadows — is all the shader's job.

GLBWhy is a .glb file so big?

A .glb is a binary package that holds everything one model needs: positions, normals, colours, the rig, all the clips, and (for non-cartoon models) texture images. The two fighters in this game are roughly 9 MB each.

This page uses the rigged .glb as the source it shows in the viewer. The actual game runtime ships a much smaller encoded form (~6 MB per fighter) that decodes at load time — same look, smaller file.

fpsWhat is "frame data"?

A frame is one still image the game draws. A typical display shows 60 of them per second. "Frame data" is the list of which frame each move hits, blocks, or ends on.

The jab in this game is 0.62 seconds long. At 60 frames per second that's 37 frames. Frame data says: hits on frame 4, recovers on frame 12, is fully safe to interrupt again by frame 16. Two players on different refresh-rate monitors see the same fight, just with smoother or choppier pictures — the timing is identical.

AiHow does the CPU opponent "think"?

It's not thinking. It's a short decision tree: how far is the other fighter? → if close, try a jab; are they attacking? → if yes, block; am I low on health? → if yes, play safe. The CPU reads the exact same frame data the human reads, so it plays by the same rules.

Difficulty just widens how often the CPU picks the best option versus a random one. On the lowest setting it picks randomly about half the time.

pipelineWhat's a "pipeline"?

A pipeline is a sequence of automated steps that turns one thing into another. The character pipeline in this game turns a photograph of a character into a rigged, animated, ready-to-punch fighter — in eight fixed steps, with a number-based check between each step so a bad result fails loudly instead of silently.

The whole point of a pipeline is that you can run it again on a new character and get the same quality, without anyone having to redo the same trial-and-error by hand.

gateWhat's a "gate"?

A gate is a number-based pass/fail check. Silhouette overlap must be at least 98.5 %. If the new fighter's silhouette doesn't match the reference drawing at least that well, the pipeline stops and the human decides what to do. No "looks fine" — a number, with a threshold.

This is what makes the pipeline trustworthy. A small local AI judging "does this look right?" is exactly the kind of fuzzy check that lets bad results slip through. Gating on a measured number does not.

ai artWas the art drawn by an AI?

The silhouettes were generated by an image-to-3D service from hand-made reference drawings. Everything else — the painted colour, the skeleton, the bones, every pose, every clip, every hit effect, every sound, the music, the announcer calls, the code, the font styling, the menus, the HUD — was authored or assembled by hand on top of those generated surfaces.

The pages and the README are explicit that this is a fictional parody and not affiliated with any real person or organisation.

The game

A standalone Vite + Three.js combat prototype with two fictional fighters, a standing-stance Lake America stage, locked 60 Hz simulation and one-shot visual ceremony. The match is frame-data driven: every move is N frames on a 144 Hz monitor and on a throttled phone. Rendering is the only thing that interpolates.

Tech stack

  • EngineThree.js (vendored, MIT) + WebGL2. No physics engine, no animation library other than Three's AnimationMixer.
  • Sim loopFixed-step accumulator at 60 Hz. Effects pull from a frame-data table in engine/frameData.js and a Match state machine.
  • CPUDecision tree with four difficulty dials. Reads the same frame data the human reads, so the AI plays by the same rules.
  • InputKeyboard, gamepad, touch. One set of chord macros reaches both the P1 keyboard and any gamepad.
  • Audio77 mono 32 kHz PCM effects, three music tracks, an announcer pool with ducking. Sound starts after a user gesture.
  • BuildVite, single CSS theme driven by COLOR_BIBLE.md tokens, no UI framework.

Only Three.js

Three.js is not a game engine. It is a renderer — a library that turns "put this mesh here, light it that way, point a camera at it" into the thousands of low-level WebGL calls a graphics card actually wants. It draws. It has no opinion about what a round is, what a hitbox does, or when a match ends.

The 30-second version.

Most browser games are built on an engine that owns the whole thing: the game loop, physics, collision, scenes, UI, audio, an editor. This one is built on a library that only knows how to draw triangles. Everything above the triangles — the fighting game — is in this repository.

That library is the entire third-party runtime. Not "few dependencies" as a figure of speech: package.json lists exactly one, and it is vendored into the source tree as a single MIT-licensed file, so the shipped game makes no network request to anything it does not own. No CDN, no off-site import map, no analytics, no webfont fetch. Vite builds it and never ships with it.

Three.js earns its place. It carries the genuinely hard graphics work:

  • DrawsWebGL2 state management, shader compilation, the render graph. WebGLRenderer plus setAnimationLoop — the only outside code that calls into this game each frame.
  • SkinsSkinnedMesh and Skeleton deform ~105k vertices against the 25-bone rig on the GPU, per fighter, per frame.
  • BlendsAnimationMixer cross-fades the 32 clips each fighter carries. The only animation code in the project not written here.
  • LightsShadow maps, ACES filmic tone mapping, physically-based materials for the stage and both fighters.
  • ComposesThe passes in render/postfx.js are Three's own addons — bloom, copy, output — wired by hand into a stack this repo controls.

What it does not do is everything an engine would have handed over. All of this is first-party:

  • Game loopThe fixed-step 60 Hz accumulator in main.js. Three supplies a frame callback and nothing more.
  • Combat1,715 lines across eight files in engine/: frame-data table, Match state machine, hit resolution, blocking, combos, meter.
  • CollisionAxis-aligned hit and hurt boxes resolved by hand. No physics engine — no Rapier, no Cannon, no Ammo.
  • InputKeyboard, Gamepad API and touch polled into one button struct, with a chord resolver for the two-button macros.
  • AudioRaw Web Audio — buffer sources, gain nodes, announcer ducking. THREE.Audio is not used.
  • InterfaceHUD, menus and round reel are DOM and one stylesheet. No React, no UI kit, no tween library.

Why one library instead of an engine

1. The dependency tree is readable in an afternoon. One direct dependency means one transitive tree, and this one has no branches. There is no 400-package node_modules under the shipped game, so there is no supply chain to audit and no postinstall script to trust. The complete list of third-party code running in a player's browser is: Three.js r170, MIT.

2. It will still run. No engine version to migrate, no editor project format to open, no proprietary runtime to keep licensed. The game is ES modules and WebGL2 — two things browsers have committed to indefinitely. Nothing here needs a company to stay in business.

3. It is the reason the combat is deterministic. This is the one that actually matters, and it is not a philosophical point. An engine owns the update tick and hands you a variable deltaTime, which quietly makes your game logic a function of how fast the machine happened to be running. Because nothing outside this repository owns the loop, the simulation could be written as a pure function instead: step(inputs) advances exactly one 60 Hz frame, and rendering is the only layer that interpolates.

What that buys.

The engine/ folder contains no Math.random, no Date.now, no performance.now and no trigonometry — nothing that can differ between two machines. A four-frame jab is four frames on a 144 Hz desktop and four frames on a throttled phone, and a whole round can be reconstructed from nothing but the button presses. That property is very hard to retrofit and nearly impossible to guarantee inside an engine that owns your tick. Here it falls out of the architecture for free.

The cost is honest, and it is the rest of this repository: roughly 8,000 lines across 57 modules doing work an engine would have shipped for you. "Only Three.js" is not a claim to have written a renderer — Three.js is carrying enormous weight, and reimplementing WebGLRenderer would be foolish. The claim is narrower and more useful: there is exactly one seam between this game and somebody else's code, it is documented, and everything on this side of it can be changed without asking anyone's permission.

Ceremony and feedback

The round reel is locked: title → versus shot → intros → round announcement → FIGHT slam → live input. KO holds impact state for 32 slow-mo frames plus a freeze before the card. Flawless, Double KO, Fatality, Draw and ordinary Victory each get a distinct end card with its own colour pair from the bible. Reduced-motion mode strips scaling slams and camera kicks but keeps the callouts.

The character pipeline

Every fighter in the roster comes out of one automated four-command pipeline. The run is fail-closed by gates — numbers, not vibes. Re-roll is cheap; signed-off results are not re-touched.

01 Reference turnaround

Front / side / back, RGBA, single connected alpha, tight crop.

02 Hitem3D two-export

Textured mesh + named parts mesh of the same sculpt. Bounds must match exactly.

gate: bounds ≤ 1 mm/m
03 Bake atlas → parts

Transfer the 8K colour atlas onto the six named anatomical parts in 3D.

gate: median ≤ 0.5 mm
04 Decimate (QEM)

Curvature- and colour-aware quadric error metric, per-part budget.

gate: IoU ≥ 98.5%, head ΔE50 ≤ 4
05 Measure joints

Joints come from the part bounds. Wrist is the only landmark searched — the two arms cross-check each other.

gate: wrist agree ≤ 3%
06 Rig & pose check

Canonical 25-bone skeleton. Pose check runs the same IK outside the browser and prints trunk clearance.

gate: clearance ∈ band
07 Encode to TypeScript

Pack positions, normals, colours, joints and weights into one Base64 stream in surfaceData.js.

no .glb fetch
08 Author clips

Keyframes on the frozen tree, never retargeted mocap. One jab first, then idle, walk, hurt, special.

Fighter lab.

The fighter lab puts the original vertex-color sculpt beside the textured GLB export: camera presets, animation scrubbing, and wireframe. It is the production proof for the bake in steps 02–03, not a second game.

The single biggest lesson is that vision QA of the mesh is the wrong place for a model. A small local VLM asked "does this look right?" is the exact component that says "looks fine" to a clay-coloured regression that ships at ΔE 104. Every gate above is a number with a threshold: silhouette IoU, ΔE50/ΔE95, trunk clearance in metres, floor penetration, left-right wrist agreement. The pipeline is automatable because the gates are floats.

The canonical skeleton

Every fighter shares the same 25-bone rig so combat clips can be authored once and inherited. The canonical tree: 

root > hips > spine > chest > neck > head > headEnd
                            > shoulderL/R > upperArmL/R > forearmL/R > handL/R > handEndL/R
            > upLegL/R > legL/R > footL/R > toeL/R

Why no GLB ships at runtime

The 9 MB rigged GLB inside site/fighters/<id> is the measurement source, not the deliverable. The runtime game ships a Three.js factory that decodes a packed surface stream and rig data from JavaScript modules. It is, structurally, code. No .glb, .bin, or texture image is fetched during play.

Taking a fighter apart

Content note.

Everything below this heading is the game's dismemberment system, running live in the viewer on the right: severed limbs, cartoon arterial blood, and organs on the floor. It only happens when you press something. If you would rather not, skip ahead to reference numbers — nothing later on depends on this section.

A fighting game that only drains a bar tells you the same story every round. This one keeps a record. The rule the whole system is built around is that a limb is never taken by the health bar alone. A region severs when it has been hit while the fighter is below that region's threshold — so the damage on screen is a record of how the round was actually fought. Beat someone to the body and the torso opens. Headhunt and the jaw goes. A fighter who never gets hit in the legs keeps them, at any health.

The 30-second version.

Two things have to agree before anything comes off: where the hit landed on the body, and how hurt the fighter already was. The hit height picks which regions it could plausibly have removed; the health threshold decides which of those are eligible at all. Neither is enough on its own, which is what stops a body blow at 20 % health from taking a head off.

The seven regions

This is the shipped table out of render/goreAtlas.js, thresholds and all. Threshold is remaining health. The two lethal regions sit at 0 % because they can only ever be the last thing that happens — an ordinary hit is never allowed to take them, so the round does not routinely end on a decapitation nobody chose. Only a KO or a finisher asks for those directly, which is what the Cut button on each card is doing.

Regions are named by the bones their geometry hangs from, matched by pattern rather than by exact name, because the roster does not agree on a skeleton: the authored fighters use this project's rig (upperArmL, forearmL, upLegR) and imported models arrive on whatever their source used. Matching on a pattern means a new model is dismemberable on arrival instead of after someone hand-writes a bone map. It also means a region can come back not rigged — neither fighter here has a jaw bone, so the jaw card marks itself and cutting it is a no-op rather than a silently retired region.

What happens on the frame a limb comes off

Four things, all driven from the same posed skeleton, so they agree about where the cut was:

  • GeometryEverything skinned to the severed bone chain stops being drawn. The stump bone is looked up on the animated skeleton, so the wound is where the limb is now, not where the bind pose left it.
  • MomentumThe limb becomes a prop carrying 55 % of the blow's force. A limb that flies off along the blow reads as caused by it; one that drops straight down reads as a bug.
  • SpillThe region's organs follow the cut, thrown a little more softly than the limb so they trail behind it instead of arriving together in a clump.
  • BleedThe stump is soaked before the limb has finished leaving. A cut that is not bleeding at its edge reads as plastic.

Nothing is actually deleted

The obvious way to remove an arm is to rebuild the index buffer without it. This does not do that. Every vertex carries a severMask — its total skin weight on the severed chain — and the fragment shader discards anything above 0.94. The fighter keeps one draw call and one index buffer all round, and putting the limb back for the next round is a fill(0) rather than a rebuild.

The seam lands in the right place for free.

Deep in the forearm every skin weight belongs to the severed chain and the mask is 1. Across the elbow it falls off exactly as the skinning blend does. The band left in between — mask between 0.02 and 0.94 — is the cut face, and the shader paints it raw meat instead of skin. Without that band a severed arm reads as a rendering glitch rather than a wound, and nobody had to author a cut cap for any of it.

The limb is the body's own vertices

At the moment of the cut, the posed positions of the removed vertices are lifted straight out of the fighter and thrown as a dense point cloud. It is the same silhouette that was attached a frame ago, which is the whole reason it is worth doing rather than throwing a generic lump. Those vertices are a point cloud and not a surface — their triangles are still indexed against the body — but as fast-moving points they read as wet tissue in motion and cost nothing to build. That is the right trade for something on screen for under a second. Once it lands it fades, and what stays behind is what actually belongs there: the blood stain, and the real severed chunk the atlas spilled alongside it.

Organs are props with real sizes

Every organ is authored against one number — span, its longest axis in metres, on a fighter a little under two of them tall. It is the single source of truth for scale: the build tool normalises each imported mesh to it, and the procedural fallback generates to it, so a real heart and a generated one are the same size. Authoring these by eye instead produced organs the size of a torso.

The viewer on this page generates its props procedurally from those same spans. The shipped game loads a baked library and falls through to exactly this generated blob for anything the library does not carry — so the system is never blocked on art, and a headless test that loads no assets still runs the whole path.

Blood goes into the mesh, not on top of it

Wounds are painted into a per-vertex attribute in bind space, so they follow every animation for free — no decal meshes, no texture copies, no per-frame work. The wound is located by sampling the posed surface for the nearest vertex to the hit, then painting that vertex's bind-space neighbourhood, which is what stops a crouching fighter's head wound landing on their jacket. Each splash gets a narrow downward tail so it reads as a trickle rather than a perfectly round paintbrush.

Debris bleeds in flight, not only where it lands — a limb that only stains its landing spot leaves a clean floor between the body and the piece, which is the tell that the two events are unrelated. Pieces get exactly one heavily damped bounce and then stay. Debris that skitters is distracting during a round it is not part of any more.

Try it

The controls are in the viewer panel on the right, and every one of them is a real entry point in the shipped system:

  • HealthDrag it and watch the cards above arm themselves. This is the only thing gating which regions an ordinary hit may take.
  • Land a hitRolls a height and a force, then runs onHit — the same path a real hit takes. Small hits only bleed. Heavy ones take something.
  • Beat downLands one every 0.42 s until there is nothing left to take. This is the honest demo: the body comes apart in the order it was beaten, and no two runs are the same.
  • CutCalls sever directly, ignoring the gate, which is what the KO and the finisher do.
  • Fatalityexplode() — every region at once, ordered extremities-inward so the body falls apart rather than the head simply vanishing first.
  • Patch upresetRound(). Masks and blood are zeroed, debris is retired, the floor is washed. A new round starts on a clean stage.
What this demo is not.

It is the shipped design, the shipped thresholds and the shipped numbers, but the viewer on this page is its own small copy of the runtime rather than the game booted in an iframe. The difference that matters: a real hit gets its height and force from the collision solver, and Land a hit rolls them. Everything downstream of that roll — which regions are eligible, what severs, where the cut lands, what spills, how it falls — is the logic described above.

One thing genuinely had to change, and it is a property of the asset rather than of the system. This page loads the 9 MB measurement GLB, which is blended much more softly than the rig the game ships: a vertex in the middle of this Trump's head is only about half bound to the head bone and nearly half to the chest, so the chain never reaches 0.94 anywhere on the head and the shipped absolute cut would leave it attached. So the mask here is scaled against the strongest hold the chain has on the fighter — what “fully attached” means is read off the rig instead of assumed — and the 0.94 and 0.02 thresholds are then applied unchanged. On a tightly rigged fighter the two are the same thing.
